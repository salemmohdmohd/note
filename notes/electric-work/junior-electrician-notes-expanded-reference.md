# Junior Electrician Notes — Expanded Reference

Study note: Always follow the electrical code adopted in your area, the
plans/specifications, equipment nameplate, manufacturer instructions, and the
AHJ/inspector.

## 1. MC Cable — Metal-Clad Cable

- MC = Metal-Clad cable
- Common in:
  - Commercial buildings
  - Offices
  - Stores
  - Industrial buildings
  - Multifamily buildings
- Can be used exposed or concealed where permitted.
- Standard non-jacketed MC is generally for dry locations.
- Special jacketed/listed MC can be used in wet or outdoor locations.

### Common MC Sizes

- **12/2 MC — typically 20A**
  - Common branch circuit.
  - Usually used for:
    - Receptacles
    - Lighting
    - General commercial circuits
  - "12" = 12 AWG wire.
  - "2" = two insulated conductors.
  - Ground is additional.
- **12/3 MC — typically 20A**
  - Three insulated conductors + ground.
  - Useful when an extra conductor is needed.
  - Examples:
    - Multi-wire branch circuits
    - Certain switch circuits
    - Circuits needing two hot conductors
    - Some 120/208V or 120/240V applications
- **10/2 MC — commonly 30A**
  - Used for larger loads when the circuit design requires it.

### MC Important Notes

- MC armor provides physical protection.
- Use the correct:
  - MC connector
  - Anti-short/bushing when required by the fitting/cable system
  - Box
  - Grounding method
- Do not assume every MC cable is suitable outdoors.
- Look at the cable markings before installation.

## 2. Romex / NM-B Cable

- Romex is a common brand name for NM-B cable.
- NM = Nonmetallic-sheathed cable.
- Primarily used for residential wiring.
- Installed in dry locations.
- Common installations:
  - Inside walls
  - Ceilings
  - Floors
  - Attics
  - Basements where permitted
- Not normally used:
  - Underground
  - In wet locations
  - Outdoors where exposed to moisture
- NM-B conductors are treated using the applicable NM-B ampacity rules, even
  though the individual conductor insulation has a higher temperature rating.

### Common Copper NM-B Sizes

**14/2 — 15A**

- Very common residential cable.
- Typical uses:
  - Lighting
  - General 15A receptacle circuits
  - Bedrooms
  - Living areas
- Common jacket color: White

**14/3 — 15A**

- Three insulated conductors + ground.
- Common when an additional conductor is needed.
- Examples:
  - 3-way switching
  - Certain multi-conductor circuits

**12/2 — 20A**

- Very common residential cable.
- Typical uses:
  - Kitchen circuits
  - Bathroom circuits
  - Garage circuits
  - Laundry circuits
  - General 20A receptacle circuits
- Common jacket color: Yellow

**12/3 — 20A**

- Three insulated conductors + ground.
- Often used when:
  - Two hot conductors are required
  - 3-way switching requires additional conductors
  - Multi-wire branch circuits are designed
- Common jacket color: Yellow

**10/2 — 30A**

- Common heavier-load cable.
- Typical possibilities:
  - Water heaters
  - Certain HVAC equipment
  - Other 30A equipment
- Common jacket color: Orange

**10/3 — 30A**

- Used when a 30A load needs:
  - Hot
  - Hot
  - Neutral
  - Ground
- Possible equipment:
  - Certain dryers
  - Appliances
  - Equipment requiring both 120V and 240V

**8/2 — 40A**

- 40A allowable ampacity for copper NM-B.
- Typical heavier-load applications.
- Possible uses:
  - Larger water heaters
  - HVAC equipment
  - Other equipment requiring a 40A circuit
- Common jacket color: Black

**8/3 — 40A**

- Three insulated conductors + ground.
- Useful when a larger 120/240V load requires a neutral.
- 40A NM-B ampacity.

**6/2 — 55A Ampacity**

- Larger residential loads.
- Copper NM-B allowable ampacity: 55A.
- Do not automatically memorize this as "always use a 60A breaker."
- Final overcurrent protection depends on:
  - Load
  - Equipment
  - Code rules
  - Termination ratings

**6/3 — 55A Ampacity**

- Hot + Hot + Neutral + Ground.
- Used for larger 120/240V loads where a neutral is required.

Southwire lists typical copper NM-B allowable ampacities as 14 AWG = 15A,
12 AWG = 20A, 10 AWG = 30A, 8 AWG = 40A, and 6 AWG = 55A.

## 3. Quick Wire Size Reference

- 14 AWG → 15A
- 12 AWG → 20A
- 10 AWG → 30A
- 8 AWG NM-B → 40A
- 6 AWG NM-B → 55A

### Easy Memory

- 14 = 15
- 12 = 20
- 10 = 30
- 8 = 40
- 6 = 55

These values are useful for copper NM-B study purposes; other wiring methods
and applications can have different allowable ampacities.

## 4. Understanding /2 and /3

### Example: 12/2 With Ground

- 12 = 12 AWG.
- 2 = two insulated conductors.
- Plus equipment ground.

Common conductors:

- Black = Hot
- White = Neutral
- Bare copper = Ground

### Example: 12/3 With Ground

- 12 = 12 AWG.
- 3 = three insulated conductors.
- Plus ground.

Common conductors:

- Black = Hot
- Red = Hot / switched hot
- White = Neutral
- Bare copper = Ground

### Important

- Ground is normally not counted in the /2 or /3 designation.

## 5. Common NM-B Jacket Colors

Common manufacturer convention:

- White jacket → 14 AWG
- Yellow jacket → 12 AWG
- Orange jacket → 10 AWG
- Black jacket → commonly 8 AWG or 6 AWG

### Remember

- Jacket color helps identify wire size.
- Conductor color tells you the intended conductor function.
- Do not confuse the two.

## 6. 18/2 Cable

- 18/2 is generally NOT standard NM-B branch-circuit wiring.
- Usually associated with low-voltage applications.
- Examples:
  - Thermostats
  - Doorbells
  - Alarm systems
  - Controls
  - Low-voltage lighting
- Do not use ordinary 18 AWG low-voltage cable as a substitute for 14/2 or
  12/2 residential branch-circuit wiring.

## 7. U.S. Wire Color Coding

### Ground / Equipment Grounding Conductor

- Green = Ground
- Green/Yellow = Ground
- Bare Copper = Ground

### Neutral / Grounded Conductor

- White = Neutral
- Gray = Neutral

### Correct Terminology

- ❌ "Cold"
- ✅ Neutral

A neutral can still be dangerous and should never be assumed safe simply
because it is white.

### Hot / Ungrounded Conductors

Common colors:

- Black = Hot
- Red = Hot
- Blue = Hot/phase in many commercial systems
- Other permitted colors may also be used.

The NEC specifically reserves white/gray for grounded conductors and
green/green-yellow for equipment grounding; it does not simply require every
hot conductor to be black.

## 8. Common U.S. Color Practices

### 120V Single-Phase

- Black → Hot
- White → Neutral
- Green/Bare → Ground

### 120/240V Single-Phase

Common residential practice:

- Black → L1
- Red → L2
- White → Neutral
- Green/Bare → Ground

### 120/208V Three-Phase — Common Trade Practice

- Black → Phase A
- Red → Phase B
- Blue → Phase C
- White → Neutral
- Green → Ground

### 277/480V Three-Phase — Common Trade Practice

Often seen as:

- Brown → Phase A
- Orange → Phase B
- Yellow → Phase C
- Gray → Neutral
- Green → Ground

These phase colors are common trade conventions; always check the building's
posted identification system instead of assuming.

## 9. Chinese / IEC-Style Cable Colors

### Modern Common Identification

- Brown → Live / Hot / Line
- Blue → Neutral
- Green/Yellow → Protective Earth / Ground

For applicable multicore cables, Chinese standards use green/yellow for
protective grounding purposes and light blue for neutral identification.

### Three-Phase IEC-Style

Common modern identification:

- Brown → L1
- Black → L2
- Gray → L3
- Blue → Neutral
- Green/Yellow → Ground / PE

### Very Important

- ❌ Do not assume black = ground.
- ❌ Do not assume yellow = ground.
- ✅ Green/Yellow is the normal protective-earth identification in modern
  IEC-style systems.
- Older Chinese equipment may use different color systems.

Always verify:

- L = Line
- N = Neutral
- PE = Protective Earth
- Equipment wiring diagram
- Manufacturer markings
- Meter/testing

## 10. Receptacle Terminal Colors

Typical U.S. receptacle:

- Brass screw → Hot
- Silver/light-colored screw → Neutral
- Green screw → Ground

Easy memory:

- Black → Brass
- White → Silver
- Green/Bare → Green

OSHA training material also identifies black-to-brass, white-to-light/silver,
and green-to-green as the normal receptacle arrangement.

## 11. Common Electrical Terms

### Hot / Line

- Carries voltage from the source to the load.

### Neutral

- Grounded circuit conductor.
- Normally carries return current in many circuits.
- Neutral is not the same thing as ground.

### Ground

- Equipment grounding conductor.
- Normally does not carry operating current.
- Provides a low-impedance fault-current path during a fault.

### Line

- Incoming/source side of a device.

### Load

- Outgoing side supplying downstream equipment.

Example on a GFCI:

- LINE → incoming power
- LOAD → protected downstream devices

## 12. 120V vs. 240V Basics

### Typical 120V Circuit

- Hot
- Neutral
- Ground

Approximately:

- Hot to Neutral → 120V
- Hot to Ground → 120V
- Neutral to Ground → ideally near 0V under normal conditions, though real
  systems can show some voltage

### Typical 240V Circuit

- Hot L1
- Hot L2
- Ground

Approximately:

- L1 to L2 → 240V

### 120/240V Circuit Requiring Neutral

- Hot L1
- Hot L2
- Neutral
- Ground

Used when equipment needs both:

- 240V loads
- 120V loads

## 13. GFCI vs. AFCI

### GFCI

- Ground-Fault Circuit Interrupter
- Primarily protects people from dangerous ground-fault current.
- Often encountered around locations with increased shock risk.

### AFCI

- Arc-Fault Circuit Interrupter
- Detects dangerous electrical arcing that can cause fires.

### Important

- Required locations depend on the electrical-code edition adopted by the
  jurisdiction.
- Do not memorize one nationwide location list without checking the local
  adopted code.

## 14. Breaker Basics

- Breaker protects the circuit conductors from excessive current.
- Common residential branch breakers:
  - 15A
  - 20A
  - 30A
  - 40A
  - 50A
  - 60A
- A larger breaker does not mean you can simply use smaller wire.
- Breaker sizing must match:
  - Wire
  - Load
  - Equipment
  - Code requirements

### Never Do This

- ❌ 14 AWG copper on a normal 20A general-purpose branch circuit
- ❌ 12 AWG copper on a normal 30A general-purpose branch circuit
- ❌ Increase a breaker because the existing breaker keeps tripping

A tripping breaker can indicate:

- Overload
- Short circuit
- Ground fault
- Equipment problem
- Wiring problem

## 15. Copper vs. Aluminum

- Copper and aluminum conductors do not always use the same size for the same
  load.
- Never assume: #8 copper = #8 aluminum
- Equipment terminals must be listed for the conductor material.

Common markings:

- CU → Copper
- AL → Aluminum
- CU/AL → Listed for copper or aluminum, when specifically marked/rated.

## 16. Solid vs. Stranded Wire

### Solid

- One solid conductor.
- Common in smaller residential NM-B.
- Holds shape well.
- Easy to terminate under many device screws.

### Stranded

- Multiple smaller strands.
- More flexible.
- Common in:
  - Commercial work
  - Conduit
  - Larger conductors
  - Equipment wiring

Southwire's NM-B construction, for example, uses solid conductors for many
#14–#10 products and stranded construction beginning with larger sizes such
as #8.

## 18. Basic Electrical Formulas

### Power

- Watts = Volts × Amps
- W = V × A

Example:

- 120V × 10A = 1,200W

### Current

- Amps = Watts ÷ Volts

Example:

- 1,500W ÷ 120V = 12.5A

### Voltage

- Volts = Watts ÷ Amps

## 19. Apprentice Safety Reminders

- Treat every conductor as energized until verified otherwise.
- Never trust wire color alone.
- Verify with appropriate test equipment.
- Follow lockout/tagout procedures where applicable.
- Verify the correct circuit before touching conductors.
- Test your tester on a known source before and after checking a circuit when
  appropriate.
- Neutral and ground are not interchangeable.
- Never replace a breaker with a larger breaker simply to stop tripping.
- Check equipment nameplates.
- Check conductor insulation and cable markings.
- Follow the plans.
- Follow manufacturer instructions.
- Follow the locally adopted NEC/code.
- When unsure, stop and verify with the supervising electrician.
